Abstract
This study explores how Territorial Intelligence (TI) has evolved between 1998 and 
2024 through a bibliometric-systematic literature review of 66 peer-reviewed articles

indexed in Scopus and Web of Science. By combining systematic review procedures 
with bibliometric analysis, the article analyses the main publication trends, thematic 
patterns, and conceptual shifts in the field. The findings show a clear growth in scien­
tific interest over the last decade, alongside a gradual move from Smart Cities towards 
Smart Regions and Smart Territories, with greater attention to sustainability, participa­
tory governance, and territorial innovation. Based on the Quintuple Helix framework, 
the article proposes an integrative conceptual model in which territorial innovation 
emerges from the interaction between academia, industry, governance, civil society, 
and the environment, leading to sustainable territorial development. The study con­
tributes to the literature by extending the Quintuple Helix perspective to low-density 
territories and by highlighting TI as a useful framework for place-based development, 
policy design, and territorial cohesion.

1	
Introduction
For several decades, low-density territories have faced structural challenges 
that compromise their economic, social, and environmental sustainability. 
Among the most relevant are: (i) depopulation, resulting in the loss of demo­
graphic and skilled human capital; (ii) economic decline, associated with a 
scarcity of employment opportunities and the fragility of the business fabric; 
and (iii) ecological vulnerability, translating into pressure on natural resources 
and the abandonment of traditional agricultural practices (Barca et al., 2012; 
Bento et al., 2021). These problems, when combined, reinforce processes 
of territorial marginalisation, widen regional inequalities, and hinder the 
implementation of effective public policies for social and territorial cohesion 
(Keryan et al., 2025).
In this context, the concept of TI gains prominence, defined as the ability 
to integrate demographic, economic, cultural, and environmental informa­
tion into collaborative decision-making processes, promoting place-based and 
innovative regeneration strategies (Girardot, 2004, 2009). TI is not limited to 
the use of data or digital technologies; rather, it involves the creation of co-
creation ecosystems where different local, regional, and transnational actors 
participate in defining development solutions adapted to the specificities of 
each territory (García-Madurga et al., 2020).

International literature has shown that territories that adopt collaborative 
governance models achieve greater resilience and attractiveness (Camagni 
and Capello, 2013; McCann and Ortega-Argilés, 2018). However, much of the 
research on “intelligence” applied to territories continues to focus on urban 
contexts (Smart Cities), leaving rural and low-population-density realities in 
the background (Figure 1), although these areas represent a significant part 
(75%) of the European territory (OECD, 2020).
While smart city research has expanded rapidly over the last decade, its 
conceptual and technological frameworks are largely designed for urban envi­
ronments characterised by high population density, advanced infrastructure, 
and concentrated innovation ecosystems (Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2012; 
Hollands, 2020). Consequently, the direct transfer of these models to rural or 
peripheral territories remains problematic, requiring conceptual adaptations 
that consider demographic dispersion, institutional capacity, and environ­
mental constraints (European Commission, 2018; Navío-Marco et al., 2020; 
OECD, 2020).

This article aims to help fill this gap by proposing a conceptual model of TI 
based on the Quintuple Helix paradigm (Carayannis and Campbell, 2010, 2011). 
This model integrates five fundamental dimensions: (i) Environment, as the 
central axis and driver of sustainable innovation; (ii) Academia, as a generator 
and disseminator of scientific knowledge; (iii) Industry, as a promoter of local 
value chains and technological innovation; (iv) Governance, responsible for 
defining multi-level public policies and financing mechanisms; and (v) Society, 
which validates and reinforces social capital through active participation and 
co-creation practices.
In this context, the present study seeks to contribute to a deeper under­
standing of TI by exploring how the concept has evolved and by positioning 
it within broader territorial innovation frameworks (Girardot, 2004, 2009; 
García-Madurga et al., 2020). More specifically, it aims to connect the expand­
ing literature on smart development approaches with the emerging perspective 
of TI, emphasing its relevance for low-density and peripheral regions, where 
innovation, governance, and sustainability must be adapted to specific territo­
rial conditions (McCann and Ortega-Argilés, 2018; OECD, 2020; Navío-Marco 
et al., 2020). To address this purpose, this article pursues three main research 
objectives: (1) Map and systematise the scientific production on TI and 
related concepts, including Smart Cities, Smart Regions, and Smart Territories 
(Tranfield et al., 2003; Donthu et al., 2021); (2) Analyse critically the conceptual 
evolution from urban-centred smart city approaches towards broader territo­
rial perspectives (Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2012; Hollands, 2020); and 
(3) Develop an integrative conceptual model grounded in the Quintuple Helix 
framework, positioning the model as a structuring axis of territorial innova­
tion in rural, peripheral, and low-density territories (Carayannis and Campbell, 
2010, 2011; Rosado-García et al., 2021).
This study is organised as follows. It opens with an introduction and theo­
retical framework on TI, followed by a description of the research methodol­
ogy. The subsequent sections present and discuss the results, and the article 
concludes with key contributions, conclusions, limitations and directions for 
future research.

Theoretical Framework
Research on territorial development has evolved in recent decades from 
models centred on economic growth to approaches that integrate social, cul­
tural, and environmental dimensions. Boisier (2003) and Bridger and Alter 
(2008) highlighted that effective development strategies must recognise local

specificities, favouring place-based approaches over uniform and undifferenti­
ated policies. In this context, concepts such as TI, Smart City, Smart Region, 
and Smart Territory have emerged, reflecting a paradigm shift in how territory 
should be conceived and governed.
2.1	
Territorial Intelligence: Origins and Evolution
The concept of TI emerged in the early 2000s, especially in the European con­
text, as a response to the need to integrate heterogeneous information into 
shared decision-making processes. Girardot (2004, 2009) defined TI as the 
ability to transform data into knowledge applicable to local and regional devel­
opment, through collaborative processes involving institutional, business, and 
community actors.
While initially TI was strongly associated with regional planning and the 
use of Geographic Information Systems (GIS), it quickly evolved into a multi­
dimensional approach, focused on the creation of knowledge co-creation eco­
systems (Camagni and Capello, 2013). This evolution has increased the value of 
territorial capital, understood as the combination of tangible resources (infra­
structure, natural heritage, productive resources) and intangible resources 
(social capital, knowledge, institutional networks) (Camagni and Capello, 2013).
Thus, TI should not be interpreted merely as a technological tool, but rather 
as an adaptive and integrative process that aims to articulate social, economic, 
cultural, and environmental dimensions, promoting territorial resilience, sus­
tainable innovation, and social cohesion (García-Madurga et al., 2020).
2.2	
From Smart Cities to Smart Territories
The scientific debate surrounding TI is closely linked to the evolution of the 
concept of smart cities, which emerged as urban laboratories for technologi­
cal innovation, widely disseminated since the 2010s (Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty 
et al., 2012). In an initial phase, Smart Cities were essentially associated with 
technological solutions focused on intelligent mobility, energy networks, digi­
tal platforms for public services, and real-time monitoring of urban activity 
(Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2012).
However, authors such as Hollands (2020) have warned of the risks of an 
overly technology-oriented approach, which could promote and exacerbate 
social exclusion and territorial inequality.
The evolution of the Smart Region and Smart Territory concepts sought to 
address these limitations, shifting the focus from cities to a broader regional 
and territorial scale (Gretzel, 2018; Navío-Marco et al., 2020). Within this frame­
work, territorial innovation, no longer restricted to the urban dimension, has 
also come to include rural and low-density areas, fostering civic participation

and inclusive governance (Roth et al., 2013), valuing cultural and identity heri­
tage (Garau, 2015), and environmental sustainability and the circular economy 
(OECD, 2020) as pillars of territorial regeneration.
Examples such as the academic and scientific study by Garau (2015) on sus­
tainable tourism in the Sardinia region (Italy) demonstrate how rural areas can 
adopt smart practices through the valorisation of cultural and identity heri­
tage, community involvement, and the use of digital technologies (augmented 
reality) to strengthen the identity and attractiveness of the territories.
These conceptual developments suggest a progressive territorial scaling of 
innovation paradigms, moving from city-based technological solutions towards 
broader territorial governance systems (Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2012; 
Gretzel, 2018; Navío-Marco et al., 2020). TI can therefore be interpreted as the 
integrative framework that operationalises this transition by combining digital 
innovation, participatory governance, and environmental sustainability, par­
ticularly in contexts where development depends on the articulation of ter­
ritorial capital, local knowledge, and multi-actor coordination (Girardot, 2004, 
2009; Camagni and Capello, 2013; García-Madurga et al., 2020).
However, the literature still presents two major limitations. First, many 
studies remain urban-centric, giving priority to city-based innovation dynam­
ics while paying comparatively less attention to rural and peripheral territories 
(Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2012; Hollands, 2020). Second, technological 
determinism often dominates smart territory and smart development initia­
tives, overshadowing the social, participatory, and environmental dimensions 
that are essential for more inclusive and sustainable territorial transformation 
(Hollands, 2020; OECD, 2020; Navío-Marco et al., 2020; García-Madurga et al., 
2020).
2.3	
The Quintuple Helix Paradigm
The Triple Helix model (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000; Galvão et al., 2019), 
centred on the interaction between University, Industry, and Governance, 
marked a turning point in the analysis of innovation ecosystems. Subsequently, 
the paradigm was expanded to the Quadruple Helix, adding Society as an 
essential actor in democratic and inclusive innovation processes (Carayannis 
and Campbell, 2010).
More recently, the Quintuple Helix (Carayannis and Campbell, 2011) adds 
the Environment helix to the model, recognising that the importance of eco­
logical sustainability is inseparable from social and technological innovation. 
This model articulates five dimensions: (i) Environment (engine of green inno­
vation and circular economy practices); (ii) Academia (generator and dissemi­
nator of scientific and technological knowledge); (iii) Industry (responsible

for boosting local value chains and business innovation); (iv) Governance 
(guarantor of place-based public policies and multi-level coordination); and 
(v) Society (agent of participation, co-creation, and social validation of imple­
mented solutions).
The inclusion of the environmental dimension gives the Quintuple Helix 
model particular relevance in the context of ecological and digital transitions, 
since the environment is understood not only as an external condition to inno­
vation processes, but as a constitutive dimension of knowledge production, 
innovation, and sustainable development. In this sense, the Quintuple Helix 
expands upon the perspective of the Triple Helix, centred on the relation­
ships between university, industry, and government, as well as the Quadruple 
Helix approach, which integrates civil society, by incorporating the natural 
environment as a structuring axis of innovation geared towards sustainabil­
ity (Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff, 2000; Carayannis & Campbell, 2010; Carayannis 
et al., 2012). This approach is particularly relevant for territorial development, 
as it allows for the articulation of endogenous resources, environmental chal­
lenges, institutional capacities, and locally based innovation processes, in line 
with broader strategic frameworks, such as the United Nations 2030 Agenda 
and the European Strategies for Smart Specialisation – RIS3 (United Nations 
General Assembly, 2015; McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 2015; Foray et al., 2018).
In this article, the academic helix is ​understood as a broad sphere of knowledge 
production, circulation, and application, encompassing universities, research 
centres, higher education institutions, laboratories, R&D units, and other knowl­
edge intermediaries. Thus, universities are not treated as an autonomous or sep­
arate category, but as central institutional actors within the broader academic 
sphere. This clarification is consistent with the Triple Helix model, which recog­
nizes the university as a central institution in knowledge production and innova­
tion, and with later approaches that emphasize more distributed, collaborative, 
applied, and transdisciplinary forms of knowledge production (Gibbons et al., 
1994; Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff, 2000; Carayannis & Campbell, 2011).
In this context, TI and the Quintuple Helix take on special importance, 
as they allow for: (i) the articulation of scientific knowledge and local know-
how; (ii) the promotion of sustainable innovation in local production chains; 
(iii) the definition of public policies adapted to territorial specificities; (iv) the 
involvement of citizens as co-producers of solutions, and (v) the positioning of 
the environment as the central axis of territorial innovation.
2.4	
Territorial Intelligence and Cohesion in Low-Density Territories
Applied to rural and peripheral regions characterised by low population den­
sity, TI based on the Quintuple Helix model is seen as a tool for territorial

regeneration. On the one hand, it promotes the integration of endogenous 
resources into innovative value chains, boosting local economies (Marques 
et al., 2019). On the other hand, it encourages the creation of participatory eco­
systems, such as Living Labs or the creation of crowdsourcing platforms, which 
increase the adaptive capacity of territories (Roth et al., 2013).
By integrating sustainability, technological innovation, and social participa­
tion, TI can promote: (i) population retention by creating economic and social 
innovation opportunities; (ii) environmental sustainability through efficient 
resource management and circular economy practices; (iii) social cohesion 
by strengthening social capital and local cooperation networks; (iv) territorial 
development through the enhancement of natural and cultural heritage as fac­
tors of tourist and economic attractiveness.
TI emerges as an integrative and adaptive paradigm, capable of guiding low-
population-density territories towards more resilient, inclusive, and sustain­
able pathways while simultaneously responding to the demands of the digital, 
ecological, and social transition.
3	
Methodology
This study adopts a bibliometric-systematic literature review (B-SLR) design, 
combining the transparency and replicability of systematic review procedures 
with the mapping capacity of bibliometric analysis (Tranfield et al., 2003; 
Donthu et al., 2021; Marzi et al., 2025). In this approach, bibliometric tech­
niques are used to identify the intellectual structure, publication patterns, and 
thematic evolution of the field, while the systematic review component supports 
the selection, screening, and interpretive synthesis of the corpus (Marzi et al., 
2018; Donthu et al., 2021). This combination is particularly suitable for concep­
tually fragmented and multidisciplinary research domains, as it enables both a 
structured mapping of the literature and a more robust conceptual interpreta­
tion of the field’s development (Tranfield et al., 2003; Donthu et al., 2021).
This combined approach is particularly useful for fragmented and multi­
disciplinary fields such as TI and smart territorial development, as it makes it 
possible to map the literature in a structured way while also supporting a more 
informed conceptual interpretation of the field’s evolution.
3.1	
Research Protocol and Data Sources
To identify the relevant scientific literature, the databases Scopus and Web 
of Science were selected as the primary data sources. These databases are 
widely recognised in bibliometric and systematic review studies due to their 


9

extensive coverage of peer-reviewed academic publications and their suitabil­
ity for quantitative bibliometric analysis (Donthu et al., 2021).
The search strategy aimed to capture publications related to TI and associ­
ated concepts such as Smart Cities, Smart Regions, and Smart Territories. In 
the Scopus database, the following search query was applied:
TITLE-ABS-KEY (“intellig* territor*”) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (“smart* terri­
tor*”) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (“smart region*”) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (“intellig* 
region*”)
The search covered the period 1998–2024, and only peer-reviewed journal arti­
cles and review articles written in English were considered. The search was lim­
ited to the following research areas: Business, Management and Accounting; 
Social Sciences; Economics, Econometrics and Finance; Arts and Humanities; 
and Environmental Sciences. This procedure resulted in 99 articles.
A similar search strategy was applied in the Web of Science database, using 
equivalent search expressions adapted to the database syntax. The search was 
limited to English-language journal articles and review articles within rel­
evant research categories, including Environmental Studies, Urban Studies, 
Geography, Management, Business, Public Administration, Regional and 
Urban Planning, Sociology, Development Studies, and related interdisciplin­
ary fields. The search returned 59 articles.
3.2	
Data Processing and Study Selection
Following the data collection phase, the bibliographic records obtained from 
both databases were exported in BibTeX format and imported into the RStudio 
environment, using the Bibliometrix package for data processing and analysis 
(Allaire, 2012; Büyükkıdık, 2022). Bibliometrix is widely used in bibliometric 
research because it enables data cleaning, merging of databases, and genera­
tion of bibliometric indicators within a reproducible analytical framework 
(Donthu et al., 2021).
After merging the Scopus and Web of Science datasets, 49 duplicate records 
were identified and removed automatically, resulting in a preliminary dataset 
of 109 articles.
Subsequently, a screening procedure based on titles and abstracts was con­
ducted in order to ensure the conceptual relevance of the selected publications. 
Studies were excluded when they (i) did not address theoretical or conceptual 
aspects related to TI or smart territorial development; (ii) focused exclusively 
on purely technical or technological aspects unrelated to territorial governance 
or development; and (iii) consisted of literature reviews on the same topic.

A little over a decade later, Roth et al. (2013), exploring two crowdsourcing 
cases in Valais and South Tyrol, highlighted that openness to the community 
and transparency in the selection of differentiating projects proved crucial 
to strengthening local innovation ecosystems. They also pointed out that 
more restrictive approaches only favoured the visibility of Small and Medium 
Enterprises (SMEs), preventing the construction of a true collective TI.
Next, Garau (2014) presented the prototype of a mobile application with 
augmented reality, designed to promote the cultural heritage of Sardinia 
(Italy). This innovative project integrated top-down decisions from public 
stakeholders and bottom-up participation from local communities, creating 
intelligent customisable routes and introducing, within a Smart Region logic, 
the idea of ​do-it-yourself experiences.
The following year, Garau (2015) expanded this analysis in Marmilla (Italy), 
arguing that sustainable rural tourism can only be achieved through cultural 
planning and systemic strategies that reconcile landscape preservation with 
the dynamics generated by the effect of globalisation. The author also proposes 
paradigms for the intelligent (Smart) use of the territory in order to strengthen 
the links between urban and rural areas, through the use of integrated man­
agement and planning technologies.
Valdez et al. (2018), criticizing the utopian narratives of roadmaps for Smart 
Cities, based on the MotionMap project in Milton Keynes (England), found 
that the concrete benefits of the project resulted primarily from the consolida­

rather than from the technological innovations themselves.

Discussion
Despite its growing relevance, TI research remains fragmented and lacks 
standardised conceptual definitions across disciplines (Girardot, 2004; García-
Madurga et al., 2020). This conceptual fragmentation reflects the interdis­
ciplinary nature of the field, which intersects with research streams such as 
smart cities, regional innovation systems, territorial governance, and sustain­
ability transitions.
5.1	
Conceptual Evolution of Territorial Intelligence
The analysis of publication trends and keywords suggests that the concept 
of TI has evolved within a broader landscape of research on smart develop­
ment, regional innovation, and territorial governance. Early discussions of 
smart development were primarily centred on urban contexts, where cities 
were considered key hubs of technological innovation, digital infrastructure, 
and knowledge-intensive activities (Caragliu et al., 2011; Batty et al., 2012). 
This urban orientation contributed to the rapid expansion of the smart city 
paradigm, which has strongly influenced subsequent debates on territorial 
innovation.
Over time, however, the literature has progressively expanded beyond the 
city scale, incorporating broader territorial perspectives such as smart regions 
and smart territories (Navío-Marco et al., 2020). This conceptual evolution 
reflects growing recognition that innovation processes are embedded in 
complex territorial systems involving multiple actors, institutions, and socio-
economic dynamics.
In this context, TI has emerged as a framework that integrates knowledge 
production, governance mechanisms, and collective decision-making pro­
cesses in territorial development (Girardot, 2004, 2009; García-Madurga et al., 
2020). Rather than focusing solely on technological solutions, TI emphasises 
the capacity of territories to mobilise information, knowledge, and coopera­
tion in order to support sustainable and inclusive development strategies.
5.2	
Environmental Dimension and Sustainability
Another important finding emerging from the bibliometric analysis concerns 
the growing prominence of sustainability-related themes in the literature. The 
increasing presence of keywords related to environment, sustainability, and 
territorial resilience suggests that the concept of TI is increasingly associated 
with sustainable development challenges (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2012; 
García-Madurga et al., 2020).


21

triple helix ﻿(2026) 1–34 | 10.1163/21971927-bja10076
This change reflects broader transformations in innovation and develop­
ment paradigms, where environmental considerations are progressively inte­
grated into territorial governance and policy frameworks. In this regard, the 
Quintuple Helix model provides a useful conceptual perspective by incor­
porating the natural environment as a dimension of innovation systems 
(Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011). According to this framework, sustainable 
development depends on the interaction between knowledge institutions, 
economic actors, governance structures, society, and environmental systems.
From this perspective, TI can be interpreted as a mechanism that supports 
the integration of environmental knowledge, digital technologies, and partici­
patory governance in territorial development strategies. Such integration is 
particularly relevant in the context of sustainability transitions, which require 
coordinated actions across multiple institutional and territorial levels (OECD, 
2020; Rosado-García et al., 2021).
5.3	
Governance and Multi-actor Innovation Systems
The results also highlight the importance of governance and multi-actor col­
laboration in the development of TI initiatives. The literature highlights that 
territorial innovation processes are not driven solely by technological infra­
structures but depend on complex interactions between public institutions, 
private organisations, academic actors, and society.
This perspective is consistent with the theoretical foundations of the helix 
models of innovation, which conceptualise innovation as the outcome of 
interactions between multiple institutional spheres. The Triple Helix frame­
work originally highlighted the relationships between universities, indus­
try, and governance, while subsequent developments extended the model to 
include society and the environment through the Quadruple and Quintuple 
Helix models (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; Galvão et al., 2019).
Within this analytical framework, TI can be understood as a governance-
oriented approach that facilitates knowledge exchange, collaborative decision-
making, and the co-creation of development strategies among territorial 
stakeholders. Such processes contribute to strengthening local capacities for 
innovation and enabling more inclusive territorial development pathways 
(García-Madurga et al., 2020).
Territorial innovation may therefore be understood as the outcome of 
interdependent and mutually reinforcing relationships among heterogeneous 
actors, rather than as the product of isolated institutional action (Etzkowitz 
& Leydesdorff, 2000; Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; Galvão et al., 2019). 
From this perspective, the proposed framework is relational rather than

merely descriptive, as it emphasises the interactive mechanisms through 
which knowledge exchange, coordination, and participation shape territorial 
development (Girardot, 2004, 2009; García-Madurga et al., 2020). This rela­
tional understanding provides the basis for the conceptual model presented in 
the following section and for the propositions derived from it.
5.4	
Implications for Low-density Territories
The findings of this study also highlight the relevance of TI for rural and low-
density territories. Traditional smart city approaches often assume the pres­
ence of dense urban infrastructures, large knowledge ecosystems, and strong 
technological capacities, conditions that are not always present in peripheral 
regions (Caragliu et al., 2011; OECD, 2020).
In contrast, TI highlights place-based development strategies that consider 
the specific characteristics of each territory, including demographic dispersion, 
institutional capacity, and environmental resources. This perspective aligns 
with contemporary approaches to regional development that stress the impor­
tance of territorial capital, local knowledge networks, and collaborative gover­
nance structures (McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 2018; OECD, 2020).
For low-density territories in particular, TI can support the design of devel­
opment strategies that combine digital innovation, environmental sustain­
ability, and community participation. By fostering cooperation between local 
actors and strengthening knowledge flows within territorial systems, this 
approach may contribute to reducing territorial disparities and promoting 
more balanced regional development trajectories (OECD, 2020; Lewandowska 
et al., 2021).

Conceptual model of Territorial Intelligence based on 
the Quintuple Helix
Building on this relational understanding, the conceptual model proposed 
below conceptualises TI as a multi-actor and multi-level process through 
which the interactions among the five helices of the Quintuple Helix frame­
work shape sustainable territorial development (Figure 9). The model syn­
thesises the main dimensions identified in the literature and illustrates how 
knowledge, governance, innovation, civil society, and the environment inter­
act in territorial development processes (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; 
García-Madurga et al., 2020).
The development of this model is grounded in three convergent signals 
emerging from the combined bibliometric mapping and interpretive synthesis

conducted in the previous sections. These signals provide the analytical and 
theoretical basis for the propositions advanced below:
	– Proposition 1. Environmental sustainability positively influences territorial 
innovation capacity in low-density territories.
	– Proposition 2. Collaborative governance strengthens knowledge exchange 
among actors in territorial innovation ecosystems.
	– Proposition 3. Multi-actor interactions within the Quintuple Helix foster 
sustainable territorial development outcomes.
These three propositions are not independent but form a cumulative and 
interdependent argument. Proposition 1 establishes the foundational condition: 
environmental sustainability is not a contextual backdrop but a constitutive 
dimension of territorial innovation capacity, particularly in low-density terri­
tories where natural resources and ecological assets represent key drivers of

endogenous development (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; García-Madurga

collaborative governance is the institutional arrangement through which

be effectively mobilised (Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff, 2000; Girardot, 2004, 2009). 
Proposition 3 then captures the systemic outcome: it is precisely through the 
structured interaction of all five helices (academia, industry, government, civil 
society, and the environment) that sustainable territorial development out­

or dimension (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; Galvão et al., 2019). Together, 
P1, P2, and P3 reflect the relational logic underpinning the proposed TI model: 
sustainability enables, governance coordinates, and multi-actor interaction 
delivers.
The proposed model operates across three analytical levels. At the macro 
level, it refers to the broader institutional and policy architecture shaping terri­
torial development, including cohesion policy, Smart Specialisation strategies 
(RIS3), rural development programmes, sustainability objectives, environmen­
tal regulation, and public funding instruments (McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 
2018; OECD, 2020). At the meso level, the model concerns territorial innovation 
ecosystems in which interactions among the helices are organised through net­
works, partnerships, and intermediary structures. At the micro level, it focuses 
on the local actors, organisations, and communities that engage in collabora­
tive processes generating knowledge, innovation, and sustainable territorial 
outcomes (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; García-Madurga et al., 2020).
Red arrows indicate macro-level policy feedback loops linking territorial 
outcomes to policy adjustment and strategic learning.
Importantly, the proposed model is not linear, but recursive in nature. 
Feedback mechanisms emerge when territorial outcomes such as innovation 
performance, social inclusion, sustainability improvements, or enhanced ter­
ritorial attractiveness are monitored, interpreted, and reintroduced into gov­
ernance processes, knowledge production, and collective decision-making, 
thereby enabling learning and adaptive territorial strategies (Girardot, 2004, 
2009; Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011). In this sense, TI operates as a 
dynamic system in which evaluation, participation, and institutional coordina­
tion continuously reshape development pathways over time (García-Madurga 
et al., 2020).
The relationships among the helices are mutually enabling and itera­
tive. Academia generates and transfers knowledge; governance coordinates, 
regulates, and supports collective action; industry translates knowledge

and as a normative driver of sustainability-oriented innovation (Etzkowitz & 
Leydesdorff, 2000; Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; García-Madurga et al., 
2020). Together, these interactions shape the capacity of territories to design 
and implement place-based development strategies (Camagni & Capello, 2013; 
McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 2018).
By positioning the Environment helix as a central driver of innovation through 
practices such as the circular economy, renewable energy development, and 
ecotourism, the model simultaneously addresses ecological sustainability chal­
lenges and demographic attractiveness. In this sense, environmental resources 
and sustainability-oriented strategies become key assets for transforming low-
population-density regions into smart, resilient, and inclusive territories.
Within this framework, the Governance helix, conceived as a multi-level 
governance system, plays a coordinating role by promoting territorial cohesion 
and inclusive policies, while ensuring the funding, regulation, and monitoring 
of smart development initiatives (Rees, 2000; European Commission, 2018).
The Academia helix contributes through the development of rural Living 
Labs and knowledge-transfer mechanisms that support innovation in micro-
enterprises, SMEs, and local communities (Camagni & Capello, 2013; Turečková 
& Nevima, 2020). These experimentation hubs in low-density territories enable 
researchers and local populations to test and co-develop technologies related, 
for instance, to precision agriculture, environmental monitoring, and digital 
services.
The Industry helix focuses on adopting solutions aligned with local value 
chains in order to strengthen the territorial economy while contributing to 
broader sustainability goals, including ESG (Environmental, Social and Gover­
nance) principles and carbon neutrality objectives (Navío-Marco et al., 2020). 
Examples include the certification and digital marketing of endogenous 
products such as honey, olive oil, or wine, as well as the development of inter-
municipal e-commerce platforms.
Finally, the Society (Community) helix plays a crucial role through active 
participation in co-creation, co-design, and crowdsourcing processes that vali­
date services and valorise the tangible and intangible cultural heritage of the 
territory (Roth et al., 2013; Garau, 2014). Such participation may involve sup­
porting cooperatives and cultural or rural associations in the management of 
shared infrastructures, such as coworking spaces and innovation workshops, 
thereby strengthening social capital within the territory. It may also include 
the use of crowdsourcing and public consultation platforms to involve com­

Contributions
7.1	
Theoretical Contribution
This study contributes to the theoretical development of TI by clarifying its 
conceptual positioning within the broader literature on smart development 
and territorial innovation systems. By systematically reviewing the scientific 
production on the topic, the study highlights how TI has evolved from earlier 
urban-centred smart city paradigms towards a more comprehensive territorial 
perspective that integrates governance, knowledge systems, and sustainability 
dimensions (Caragliu et al., 2011; Navío-Marco et al., 2020).
Additionally, the article advances the conceptual understanding of TI by 
proposing an integrative framework grounded in the Quintuple Helix model. 
By explicitly incorporating the environmental dimension as a structuring axis 
of innovation systems, the model contributes to bridging the literature on 
territorial development with sustainability transitions and multi-actor inno­
vation systems (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011; García-Madurga et al., 
2020; Rosado-García et al., 2021). In doing so, the study offers a conceptual 
lens through which TI can be interpreted as a governance-oriented approach 
capable of articulating technological innovation, participatory processes, and 
environmental sustainability within territorial development strategies.
7.2	
Methodological Contribution
From a methodological perspective, this research contributes by combining 
systematic literature review procedures with bibliometric analysis in order 
to map the intellectual structure and thematic evolution of the TI field. This 
mixed methodological approach enables the identification of key publication 
trends, influential authors, and emerging research themes, while also sup­
porting a deeper conceptual synthesis of the literature (Tranfield et al., 2003; 
Donthu et al., 2021).
By analysing a corpus of 66 peer-reviewed articles indexed in the Scopus 
and Web of Science databases, the study provides a structured overview of the 
academic development of TI between 1998 and 2024. The use of bibliometric 
tools such as the Bibliometrix package allows the visualisation of thematic pat­
terns and conceptual clusters, facilitating a systematic understanding of the 
field’s evolution and research gaps.

7.3	
Policy and Practical Contribution
Finally, the study offers relevant implications for policymakers and practi­
tioners involved in territorial development strategies. The conceptual model 
proposed in this research highlights the importance of multi-actor collabora­
tion and governance coordination within territorial innovation ecosystems. In 
particular, the Quintuple Helix framework emphasises the need to integrate 
academic knowledge, industrial innovation, governance mechanisms, civil-
society participation, and environmental sustainability in the design of ter­
ritorial development policies (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011).
These insights are particularly relevant for low-density and peripheral ter­
ritories, where traditional smart city models may not adequately address local 
development challenges. By adopting a TI perspective, policymakers may 
design place-based strategies that combine digital innovation, environmental 
sustainability, and community participation, contributing to more inclusive 
and resilient territorial development pathways (McCann & Ortega-Argilés, 
2018; OECD, 2020).

Conclusions
This study aimed to systematically analyse the scientific literature on TI in 
order to understand its conceptual evolution and to position it within broader 
territorial innovation and sustainability discussions. By combining a system­
atic literature review with bibliometric analysis, the research mapped the 
main publication trends, thematic orientations, and intellectual structures 
influencing the development of this emerging field.
The results reveal that the literature on TI has evolved in close connection 
with the broader research streams on smart cities, regional innovation systems, 
and territorial governance. Initially influenced by urban-centred smart city 
paradigms, the field has progressively expanded towards more comprehensive 
territorial approaches that integrate technological innovation, governance 
mechanisms, and sustainability concerns (Caragliu et al., 2011; Navío-Marco 
et al., 2020). This transition reflects a growing recognition that innovation 
processes are embedded in complex territorial ecosystems involving multiple 
actors, institutions, and socio-environmental dynamics.
The bibliometric analysis also highlights the increasing prominence of 
sustainability-related themes, suggesting that TI is progressively aligned with 
contemporary discussions on sustainability transitions and place-based devel­
opment strategies. In this sense, the Quintuple Helix framework provides a

useful conceptual lens to understand how interactions between academia, 
industry, governance, civil society, and the environment can support more inte­
grated territorial development processes (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011).
Building on these insights, the article proposes a conceptual model of TI 
grounded in the Quintuple Helix framework. The model emphasises the role of 
environmental sustainability as a structuring dimension of territorial innova­
tion systems and highlights the importance of collaborative governance and 
multi-actor participation in influencing sustainable territorial development 
pathways.
From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to clarifying the con­
ceptual foundations of TI and its relationship with smart territorial develop­
ment paradigms. Methodologically, the combination of systematic literature 
review and bibliometric analysis provides a structured overview of the aca­
demic development of the field between 1998 and 2024. From a policy per­
spective, the proposed framework highlights the relevance of TI as a strategic 
approach for designing place-based development policies capable of inte­
grating digital innovation, environmental sustainability, and community 
participation.
These insights are particularly relevant for low-density and peripheral ter­
ritories, where traditional smart city models may not adequately address local 
development challenges. By fostering cooperation between territorial actors 
and strengthening knowledge exchange within innovation ecosystems, TI may 
support the transformation of rural and peripheral regions into more resilient, 
inclusive, and sustainable territories.

Limitations
Despite its contributions, this study presents several limitations that should 
be acknowledged. First, the literature review was restricted to publications 
indexed in the Scopus and Web of Science databases, which, although widely 
used in bibliometric studies, may exclude relevant contributions published in 
other databases or grey literature sources. Consequently, some potentially rel­
evant studies on TI or related territorial innovation approaches may not have 
been captured in the analysed corpus.
Second, the analysis focused exclusively on English-language journal arti­
cles and review papers, which may limit the inclusion of relevant research 
produced in other languages, particularly in regions where TI initiatives are 
strongly linked to local development policies and regional governance practices.


29
Territorial Intelligence
triple helix ﻿(2026) 1–34 | 10.1163/21971927-bja10076
Third, the bibliometric approach adopted in this study highlights publica­
tion patterns and thematic structures, rather than the in-depth empirical anal­
ysis of individual case studies. While bibliometric techniques are useful for 
identifying trends, influential contributions, and conceptual structures within 
a research field (Donthu et al., 2021), they do not fully capture the contextual 
and institutional dynamics that shape TI initiatives in specific territories.
Finally, although the study proposes a conceptual model grounded in the 
Quintuple Helix framework, the model remains conceptual in nature and has 
not yet been empirically tested. Future empirical research is therefore neces­
sary to validate and operationalise the proposed framework in different territo­
rial contexts (Carayannis & Campbell, 2010, 2011).

Future Research Directions
Building on the limitations identified above, several avenues for future research 
can be highlighted.
First, future studies could expand the scope of the literature review by 
including additional databases, conference proceedings, policy reports, and 
grey literature sources. Such an expanded dataset could provide a more com­
prehensive understanding of how TI is conceptualised and implemented 
across different geographical and institutional contexts.
Second, further research should focus on empirical case studies of TI ini­
tiatives, particularly in rural and low-density territories. Comparative studies 
across different regions could provide valuable insights into how multi-actor 
governance systems, innovation ecosystems, and environmental sustainability 
strategies interact in practice.
Third, additional research could explore the operationalisation of the 
proposed conceptual model, testing the relationships between the different 
helices of the Quintuple Helix framework and their impact on territorial inno­
vation and sustainable development outcomes. Such studies could employ 
mixed methodological approaches combining qualitative case studies, social 
network analysis, and regional innovation indicators.
Fourth, future investigations may also examine the role of digital transfor­
mation and smart technologies in supporting TI initiatives, particularly in 
peripheral regions where digital infrastructures and knowledge networks play 
an important role in reducing territorial disparities.
Finally, further research could explore the relationship between TI and place-
based regional development policies, including the role of smart specialisation
