A B S T R A C T  
Financial intermediation drives resource allocation in the economy, which can influence the carbon emission 
intensity of economic output (CO2gdp). This study examines the impact of bank credit allocation on CO2gdp in 
African economies during the period 1995–2018. Two policy scenarios are empirically evaluated, taking into 
account the behaviour of financial intermediaries with limited financial resources for credit supply to the pro­
ductive sectors. Policy Scenario I, a credit allocation system that places a greater emphasis on financing de­
mands of government and state-owned enterprises (GSEs), has a positive (increasing) effect on CO2gdp. The 
alternative policy scenario, Policy Scenario II, which places a greater emphasis on financing demands of private 
sector entities (PSUs), has a negative (decreasing) effect on CO2gdp with stronger impact in the more carbon- 
intensive economies. In addition, renewable energy consumption makes greater contribution to reducing 
CO2gdp under Policy Scenario II. By implication, more credit supply to the PSUs strengthens the link between 
resource allocation and economic efficiency, resulting in the creation of greener economic output. Thus, the 
development of financial intermediation could play a role in helping African economies avoid carbon-intensive 
path to economic growth.   
1. Introduction 
The United Nations through Sustainable Development Goal 13 (UN 
SDG 13: Climate Action) has refocused global attention on the transition 
to a low-carbon economy as a key condition for ensuring that economic 
targets are sustainable. As in other developing continents, economic 
strategies in African economies are designed under the neoclassical 
argument that environmental impacts will be minimal and, as such, 
should be considered after the economic target of high per capita gross 
domestic product (GDP) has been achieved. Over the past six decades, 
Africa has experienced increasing economic output. According to the 
World Bank, Sub-Saharan Africa’s per capita GDP increased by over 45 
per cent between 1960 and 2018, rising from 1125.41 in constant 2015 
US$ in 1960 to US$1658.65 in 2018 (WDI, 2022). The North African 
countries also achieved increased economic output. For example, 
Egypt‘s per capita GDP increased from US$762.13 to US$3831.20 (WDI, 
2022). Hopes for sustained growth in GDP per capita could be jeopar­
dized by the growing carbon footprint of the economies. The estimates 
of national carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions show that in 2018, 
sub-Saharan Africa emitted 0.47 kg of CO2 per 2015 US$ of GDP (WDI, 
2022). Estimates are higher for North African countries, Algeria, Egypt, 
Morocco, Tunisia and Libya at 0.86 kg, 0.65 kg, 0.61 kg, 0.66 kg and 
1.49 kg, respectively. On average, the global economy emitted 0.41 kg of 
CO2 per 2015 US$ of GDP in 2018 (WDI, 2022). By the standard of the 
world average, African economies could be considered more carbon 
intensive. While the global economy reduced the carbon intensity of 
GDP by 47.1 per cent, sub-Saharan Africa achieved only a 9 per cent 
reduction over the period 1960–2018, and in some of the North African 
countries, the carbon intensity of GDP increased by more than 100 per 
cent (e.g., in Algeria) (WDI, 2022). 
As African countries continue to take steps to grow their economies, 
ensuring that environmental protection is inclusive of the continent’s 
needs and priorities will be crucial to ensuring that future emissions 
from the continent do not outstrip progress made by other countries (Lin 
and Agyeman, 2021). A key consideration will be the role of financial 
intermediaries (banks and financial institutions that accept transferable 
deposits) in strengthening the links between resource allocation, eco­
nomic efficiency and environmental sustainability. Among other roles, 
the financial intermediary sector mobilizes savings and allocates these 
savings to other economic entities (Abrar et al., 2021; Umar et al.,

mand in the economy changes as financial resources are withdrawn from 
the surplus units to finance productive sectors (Umar et al., 2021; Wen 
et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). As a result, the financial intermediary 
sector may play an important role in constraining investment in 
resource-intensive infrastructure and technology. Banks in several 
countries have used variable interest rates and structured loan packages 
to limit funding for substantially polluting investment operations and 
household demand for energy-intensive consumption items (Wen et al., 
2021). Their expanding responsibility in this regard also includes 
increased support for research and development in environmentally 
friendly technologies. Recent evidence also suggests that banks increase 
firms’ ability to develop technologically and achieve technical effi­
ciencies through credit services (Wang et al., 2021). 
It is unlikely that decisions of financial intermediaries regarding 
credit allocation will align with the environmental sustainability target 
in all policy contexts (Cull and Xu, 2000; Dong et al., 2020; Lamperti 
et al., 2021). One key reason is that the economic and environmental 
goals of various economic entities, both public and private, differ 
significantly (Cull and Xu, 2000; Dong et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). As 
a result, financial intermediaries would have to decide how to structure 
their credit allocation; whether a credit allocation system that prioritizes 
credit requests from private sector economic units (PSUs) over requests 
from the government and state-owned enterprises (GSEs) would be more 
socially, environmentally, and financially sustainable, with the overall 
goal of reducing the environmental impacts of human demands (Yang 
et al., 2021). The divergent economic interests of public and private 
entities create competition for the limited bank credit resources (Dong 
et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). As the engine of the market system, the 
private sector encourages competition among economic units, making 
them more productive and capable of boosting economic efficiency and 
innovations in environmentally-friendly technology (Mo, 2018; Mauli­
dia et al., 2019; Sloan et al., 2019). However, in the majority of African 
economies, GSEs represent a key component of economically dominant 
industries. Due to the political links between GSEs and the government, 
GSEs benefit from preferential policies (Firth et al., 2009; Li et al., 2012; 
Dong et al., 2020). Not surprisingly, GSEs have had increased access to 
bank credit resources in the majority of African economies in recent 
years (see GFD, 2021). 
Building on the preceding discussion, this study examines how the 
structure of bank credit allocation affects the carbon emission intensity 
of economic growth in Africa. There are two key areas where this study 
extends existing studies and implicitly contributes to the literature. One, 
this study derives policy pathways for low-carbon growth in Africa by 
relying on the structure of credit resource allocation. Under Policy 
Scenario I, the credit allocation system places a greater emphasis on 
financing government and state-owned enterprises. In an alternative 
policy scenario (Policy Scenario II), the credit allocation system priori­
tizes financing private sector units. These policy paths invariably entail 
the use of bank credit allocation to determine the environmental impacts 
of PUSs and GSEs (Yang et al., 2021). The literature on this aspect of 
economic and environmental policy paths is still scanty. In fact, related 
studies on the topic have only been able to explore the Chinese economy, 
partly because of its long history of government engagement in eco­
nomic activities (see Dong et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). Therefore, this 
present study further enriches the literature on the topic by examining 
African economies. According to the Freedom Heritage, African econo­
mies are among the most economically repressed economies and have 
strong presence of government intervention in economic activities (see 
Miller et al., 2022). Two, existing related studies have separated the 
economic and environmental dimensions of sustainable development 
(SD), using aggregate economic output to model the economic dimen­
sion and carbon emissions to model the environmental dimension. It 
follows that conflicting policy pathways have been proposed in the 
literature. In light of this, this study integrates the economic and envi­
ronmental dimensions of SD in an innovative way by employing a 
concept that defines CO2 emissions in terms of per $1 of GDP created, 
which, according to the United Nations Economic and Social Commis­
sion (UN ESCAP), tracks the synergies and trade-offs between the 
environmental and economic dimensions of SD (UN, 2009). 
The insights gained from this study can inform various stakeholders 
on how to strengthen the links between financial intermediation, 
resource allocation and environmental sustainability. Monetary au­
thorities such as central banks and financial intermediaries (commercial 
banks and other deposit-taking financial institutions) can learn how to 
transform credit creation and related policy decisions into tools for 
building sustainable economic productive capacity. The results can be 
used by multilateral development institutions such as the World Bank 
and the African Development Bank to identify critical areas for future 
action regarding resource allocation in Africa. 
The remaining part of the study is discussed in the sections that 
follow: theoretical mechanism and related empirical literature, empir­
ical methodology, results, conclusion, and policy remarks. 
2. Theoretical mechanism and related empirical literature 
In the economics of banking and finance, the credit policies of 
financial intermediaries provide the mechanism for linking mobilised 
financial resources to the real economy, where entities that engage in 
the production of goods and services interact (Gertler and Kiyotaki, 
2010). In the illustration in Fig. 1, these productive entities are cat­
egorised into two units; government and state-owned enterprises (GSEs) 
and private sector units (PSUs). These two units are economically 
motivated by diverse interests. Whereas PSUs are owned by entrepre­
neurs or shareholders and, as such, operate to achieve economic gains 
for them, GSEs are funded by public revenue (e.g., taxation) and are 
controlled predominantly by political forces rather than market forces 
(Boyne, 2002). Boyne (2002) reviewed evidence from 34 studies and 
found that GSEs have weaker organizational commitment than PSUs. 
That notwithstanding, Dong et al. (2020) argued that the diverse in­
terests among GSEs and PSUs create intense competition for limited 
bank credit resources. This argument has been elaborated by Yang et al. 
(2021) and Larsen and Oehler (2022). The political ties between GSEs 
and the government mean GSEs benefit from favourable policies. PSUs 
rely on signals from market forces and policies set by banks and mon­
etary authorities to shape their demand for credit (Firth et al., 2009; Li 
et al., 2012; Dong et al., 2020). In the face of limited financial resources 
for lending, financial intermediaries must choose between two policy 
alternatives, each of which has the potential to expand the scale of 
economic activity or promote structural and technological changes in 
the economy: 
• Policy Scenario I: a credit allocation system that gives a high pri­
ority to GSEs. This limits credit resources that PSUs can access in the 
banking system.  
• Policy Scenario 1I: a credit allocation system that gives a high 
priority to PSUs. This reduces the amount of financial resources 
available to the banking system for lending support to GSEs. 
The two policy scenarios may have different environmental impacts. 
Because of government influence, GSEs are projected to be subject to 
stronger environmental protection regulations (Yang et al., 2021). As a 
result, they are thought to be more capable of demonstrating a greater 
readiness to safeguard the environment through bank credit options 
(Yang et al., 2021). This line of thought also assumes that, because 
private enterprises are obligated to meet their owners’ profit demands, 
they may develop little interest in mitigating environmental concerns 
due to the additional costs involved. Policy Scenario II puts more 
emphasis on increasing private sector involvement in economic activity 
and reducing the economic participation of GSEs in the economy. The 
rationale for this policy consideration is that a credit allocation system 
that puts greater emphasis on financing GSEs (e.g., Policy Scenario I)

According to Ockwell et al. (2018), the private sector is the leading 
actor driving the development and diffusion of clean technologies in 
Africa. It thus means that industrial structure upgrading, investment in 
R&D, and technological innovation, which are core channels through 
which credit resource allocation generates low-carbon output, will be 
affected by policy choices that limit the credit resources that PSUs can 
access in the banking system, such as the one formulated under Policy 
Scenario I. This view is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. 
(2021) and a number of other studies in the literature. For example, 
Chang et al. (2019) showed that credit policy and financial constraints 
have a significant impact on R&D investments in renewable energy 
technologies. Hu et al. (2020) showed that a significant relationship 
exists between credit policies that support low-carbon technologies and 
industrial structure upgrading. Zhang et al. (2021) extended these an­
alyses to show that such a credit policy strategy will also mitigate CO2 
emissions. In another recent analysis, Khan et al. (2021) showed that 
credit constraints inhibit technology and non-technology-related inno­
vation in the private sector in developing economies. There are other 
studies that have examined how access to credit resources shapes the 
low-carbon path to growth (see Polzin et al., 2021; M´esonnier, 2022; 
Zhang et al., 2022). However, only a small number of these studies 
specifically raised questions similar to the implications highlighted by 
the two policy scenarios under consideration in this study. 
Lamperti et al. (2021) used a macro-financial agent-based model to 
examine a series of policy scenarios for the links between CO2 emissions, 
credit policies, and economic dynamics. The findings revealed that 
financial constraints intensify the economic impact of climate shocks, 
whereas policies that encourage the provision of credit to firms increase 
economic productivity by facilitating investments in low-carbon capital 
goods. Given the diverse interests of GSEs and PSUs, the majority of the 
other related studies focused on the Chinese economy, where govern­
ment support for GSEs influences bank credit allocation. Dong et al. 
(2020) showed that the structure of bank credit allocation in China fa­
vours significantly heavy and polluting industries, and that by giving 
preference 
to

private-sector-owned. In another study, Yang et al. (2021) used spatial 
econometric methodologies to evaluate the impact of credit allocation 
structures on CO2 emissions in China and found that the credit allocation 
system adds significantly to CO2 emissions when greater emphasis is 
placed on financing PSUs. The analysis found no evidence that bank 
lending to GSEs crowds out more productive private sector investment 
in energy efficiency and other low-carbon technologies. Instead, the 
study found that GSEs are becoming more environmentally responsible 
and that increasing their access to credit resources boosts 
high-social-return initiatives like renewable energy infrastructure, with 
a knock-on effect on low-carbon growth strategies. 
The dominant presence of GSEs in all African economies, as well as 
the growing need to encourage economic diversification on the conti­
nent through private sector-oriented economic policies, mean that this 
study can make a valuable contribution to the literature. The goal is to 
explore policy strategies on how financial intermediation could 
strengthen the links between resource allocation and low-carbon growth 
trajectories. 
3. Empirical methodology 
3.1. Theoretical framework and model specification 
The concept of low-carbon growth, as defined in Equation (1), is 
measured by the carbon intensity of economic output. In line with the 
literature, GDP is used as the measure of aggregate economic output. 
The concept entails, on one hand, reducing environmental impacts 
while, on the other hand, allowing economic activity to grow (i.e., 
reducing CO2 emissions per unit of GDP). As a yardstick for under­
standing the linkages between economic activity and environmental 
impacts, CO2 emissions per unit of GDP evaluate economic policies to 
assist policymakers in improving the eco-efficiency of the economy (UN, 
2009).

↑Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
= ↓CO2 per unit of GDP
(1) 
The path to achieving low-carbon output could be shaped by a 
number of factors. One of these factors is the level of economic devel­
opment. According to the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) hypoth­
esis, rising per capita income increases CO2 emissions in the early stages 
of development as structural transformations stimulate aggregate output 
through energy-intensive industrial production, but reduces CO2 emis­
sions in later stages when the economy structurally shifts to techno­
logically driven light-industry sectors (Tenaw and Beyene, 2021). To 
account for the EKC hypothesis, per-capita GDP is included in the model, 
both on its own and in its squared term. Further specification consid­
erations are made, taking into consideration that African economies are 
characterized by an undiversified energy sector and a heavy reliance on 
natural resources (UN, 2016). Thus, renewable energy and natural 
resource extraction are introduced as additional variables in the model. 
Given the importance of diversifying African economies, it is critical to 
recognize how these dimensions of economic structure shape the con­
tinent’s actions towards the low-carbon growth path. The UN SDG 7 
target 2 recommends increasing the use of renewable energy as part of 
the strategies for reducing the carbon intensity of economic output. 
According to Pehl et al. (2017), solar, wind, and other renewable energy 
sources create an insignificant carbon footprint compared to traditional 
fossil fuels (oil, coal, and gas). Also, natural resource utilization in Af­
rican economies has been accompanied by high energy and material 
intensities, as well as waste generation (UN, 2016). These factors can 
undermine economic progress by intensifying carbon emissions. 
Together, the introduction of these variables into the model is aimed at 
broadening insights for policy decisions. Based on these considerations, 
the following equation is formulated to guide the empirical 
investigation:

natural resource extraction. φ0 is the intercept of the functional rela­

index t. The “ln” symbol indicates that all the variables are defined in

3.2. Sample selection and data sources 
This study uses balanced panel data from 34 African countries 
selected based data availability from 1995 to 20181 (see Fig. 2). To 
measure per capita income ((PCgdp), GDP per capita in constant 2015 
US$ is used, renewable energy consumption (RE) is calculated as a 
percentage of total final energy consumption, while natural resource 
extraction (NR) is defined in the form of derived rents as a percentage of 
GDP. Data on these variables are from the World Development In­
dicators (WDI) database compiled by the World Bank. The Global 
Financial Development (GFD) database compiled by the World Bank 
distinguishes credit supply by deposit money banks to government and 
state owned enterprises (GSEs) from credit provided to the private sector 
(PSUs). These estimates are calculated relative to GDP. Based on these 
estimates, BCGSE is defined as the share of credit supply to GSEs in the 
banking system’s total credit supply. In the same way, BCPSU is derived 
as the share of credit supply to PSUs in the banking system’s total credit 
supply. Table 1 contains a summary description of the variables:

address estimation issues that can affect the validity of the derived

policy considerations, the MMQReg technique which takes distribu­

4.2. Results from panel regression techniques 
The derived parameter estimates are summarized in Tables 7 and 8. 
Specifically, estimates in Table 7 evaluate the appropriateness of Policy 
Scenario I while those in Table 8 assess Policy Scenario II. The FE-Reg 
with DK in column 1 models the mean effect of the explanatory vari­
ables. The MMQReg estimates for distributional heterogeneity in the 
effects of both policy scenarios in the selected African countries are 
given in columns 2–8. In Tables 7 and 8, three groups of quantiles are 
defined: the lower quantile (qtile_10th and qtile_25th); the median 
quantile (qtile_50th); and the upper quantile (qtile_75th and qtile_90th). 
In Table 7, the coefficient of lnBCGSE is positive and statistically 
significant. The FE-Reg with DK in column 1 shows that a 1% increase in 
BCGSE will have a 0.037% increase in CO2gdp. The MMQReg estimates 
in columns 2–8 show that a 1% increase in BCGSE increases CO2gdp 
irrespective of the quantile location of a country in the distribution, 
specifically by 0.036% at the 10th quantile and by 0.038% at the 90th 
quintile (see the quantile regression plots in Fig. 3). These estimates 
offer a different policy suggestion regarding the carbon-intensity effect 
of economic activity of the GSEs when compared to what Yang et al. 
(2021) observed in China. Clearly, the hypothesis that GSEs generate 
less carbon-intensive output with bank credit offerings due to direct 
government influence is rejected. Rather, the financial intermediary 
system generates significant economic inefficiency and higher CO2 
emissions in per $1 of GDP when credit allocation policies lend more to 
GSEs. Hence, Policy Scenario I is not a low-carbon path to growth. 
Conversely, the coefficient of lnBCPSU in Table 8 is negative and sta­
tistically significant, and indicates that a 1% increase in BCPSU reduces 
CO2gdp by 0.122% (based on FE-DK in column 1). The MMQReg esti­
mates (see columns 2–8) suggest a smaller marginal impact of about 
0.099% at the 25th quantile and a higher marginal impact of 0.159% at 
the 90th quantile (see the quantile regression plots in Fig. 4). These 
estimates indicate that the financial intermediary system improves 
economic efficiency and consequently generates less carbon-intensive 
output (higher growth in GDP than in CO2 emissions) when credit pol­
icies allocate more financial resources to the private sector. Thus, Policy 
Scenario II is the appropriate policy target for avoiding the 
carbon-intensive path to growth in Africa. 
The coefficient estimates for lnPCgdp are positive but only 
statistically significant across the lower and median quantiles (i.e., 
qtile_10th-qtile_50th). The quadratic term, lnPCgdpSQ, has a statisti­
cally significant negative coefficient across all the quantiles. Together, 
the EKC’s predicted inverted U-shaped curve between environmental 
impacts and per capita GDP is confirmed at the lower and median 
quantiles but not at the upper quantiles of CO2gdp. At the upper 
quantiles, lnPCgdpSQ remains negative and statistically significant. In 
other words, economic growth is predicted to increase CO2gdp in Af­
rican economies in the early phases of development and will require 
attaining a certain level of income to bend the curve and induce a change 
away from a carbon-intensive growth pattern and toward a more 
resource-efficient and environmentally friendly growth pattern. This 
discovery is in agreement with the finding of Iorember et al. (2020), who 
documented similar finding for Nigeria, but contradicts the findings of 
Arogundade et al. (2022), who identified a U-shaped curve for some 
selected African countries. The coefficient of lnRE is negative and sta­
tistically significant across all the quantiles. A 1% increase in RE reduces 
CO2gdp by 0.394% at the 10th quantile and by 0.572% at the 90th 
quantile (see Table 8). Thus, increasing the amount of renewables in the 
energy mix generates greener economic output. This means the UN SDG 
7.2 target is a necessary condition for avoiding a carbon-intensive path 
to growth in all African countries, especially in the more 
carbon-intensive economies such as South Africa, Lesotho, and Algeria. 
The coefficient of lnNR is positive and statistically significant at the 
upper quantiles (see columns 7 and 8). This indicates that the negative 
environmental effects of relying on natural resource extraction outweigh 
its economic benefits in Africa. 
4.3. Further discussion of the empirical results 
The key findings, as illustrated in Fig. 5, show that the two policy 
scenarios have different effects on CO2gdp in the selected African 
countries. Policy Scenario I, a credit allocation system that places 
greater emphasis on financing GSEs, has a positive (increasing) effect on 
CO2gdp, indicating that CO2 emissions increase at a higher rate than 
GDP and, consequently, carbon-intensive outputs are created. The 
alternative policy path, Policy Scenario II, which places greater 
emphasis on financing PSUs, has a negative effect on CO2gdp, which 
indicates a decreasing effect on CO2 emissions per $1 of GDP. Also, 
Policy Scenario II offers greater support for greening economic output in 
the upper quartile countries, such as South Africa, Lesotho, and Algeria. 
Clearly, Policy Scenario I creates limited access to bank credit support 
for PSUs (Cull and Xu, 2000). As such, the links between resource 
allocation and the capacity to generate greener output are weakened 
(Larsen and Oehler, 2022). There are a number of interesting insights in 
the literature on how Policy Scenario II creates low-carbon growth 
paths. One is the linkages that exist between credit to the private sector 
and structural transformations in the real sector (Hu et al., 2020; Zhang 
et al., 2021). Inducing structural transformations such as industry 
structure upgrading can be a source of growth in low-carbon output 
(Zhang et al., 2021). Two is the contribution of bank credit resources to 
the growth of R & D, innovations in technical capacities and clean en­
ergy technologies (see Ling et al., 2020; Khan et al., 2021). The growth 
of solar photovoltaic (PV) technology in Africa, for instance, has in 
recent years depended on private sector investment (Ockwell et al., 
2018). Hence, allocating more credit resources to the PSUs can stimulate 
these channels that link resource allocation to growth in greener eco­
nomic output (Pu and Yang, 2022). 
Competition for bank credit resources has increased in most African 
countries in recent years, with GSEs representing a major part of the 
economically dominant industries, especially in resource-intensive sec­
tors such as mining, construction, oil and gas, and utilities. Because of 
the political links between GSEs and the government, GSEs are sup­
ported with preferential policies (Firth et al., 2009; Li et al., 2012; Dong 
et al., 2020). This helps to explain why environmental policies and 
regulations in the mining, construction, and utilities supply sectors have

instance, Shittu et al. (2021) showed that natural resources mitigate 
environmental degradation in the Asian economies, while Nathaniel 
et al. (2021) showed they intensify emissions in the BRICS economies. 
The results from this present empirical analysis suggest that economic 
reliance on natural resources generates carbon-intensive output in some 
African countries, specifically in the upper quantile countries such as 
South Africa, Lesotho, and Algeria. It is therefore likely that PSUs will 
have a role to play in diversifying the composition of economic activity 
in Africa. 
5. Conclusion and policy remarks 
Little is currently known about how banks through credit policies 
shape the linkages between resource allocation and the carbon emission 
intensity of economic output (CO2gdp). This study contributes to filling 
this gap in the literature by examining the impact of bank credit allo­
cation (BCA) on CO2gdp in a panel of 34 African economies over the 
period 1981–2018, accounting for possible distributional heterogeneity. 
Two policy scenarios were examined, taking into consideration the 
behaviour of financial intermediaries under limited financial resources 
for credit supply to productive sectors. Policy Scenario I, a credit 
allocation system that places greater emphasis on financing government 
and state-owned enterprises (GSEs), has a positive (i.e., increasing)

effect on CO2gdp. The alternative policy scenario, Policy Scenario II, 
which places greater emphasis on financing private sector entities 
(PSUs), has a negative (decreasing) effect on CO2gdp with a stronger 
impact in the more carbon-intensive economies. The EKC’s inverted U- 
shaped curve was validated in the link between CO2gdp and per capita 
GDP, while renewable energy makes a greater contribution to reducing 
CO2gdp under Policy Scenario II. Also, economic reliance on natural 
resources generates carbon-intensive output in some countries, specif­
ically in those at the upper quantiles such as South Africa, Lesotho, and 
Algeria. 
The above findings have some policy implications, particularly in the 
context that financial intermediaries can strengthen the links between 
resource allocation and environmental sustainability by shaping the 
economic and environmental goals of public and private economic en­
tities through credit policy decisions. At least three crucial consider­
ations can be underlined for African economies: (i) Policies that 
prioritise credit resource allocation to GSEs result in higher CO2 emis­
sions in per $1 of GDP, indicating a carbon-intensive path to growth. The 
recommended policy option is to allocate more credit resources to the 
private sector, which generates economic output with lower CO2 emis­
sions and hence provides a low-carbon growth path. By implication, 
Policy Scenario II defines a development path that integrates environ­
mental and economic sustainability goals (ii) Increasing renewables (e. 
g., solar energy, bio-fuels, and wind energy). in the energy mix across 
the continent is critical to avoiding a carbon-intensive path to growth, 
and as the findings of this analysis show, the economic and environ­
mental benefits may increase as the private sector takes dominance of 
economic activity. Thus, the growth of renewable energy technologies 
such as solar photovoltaic (PV) in Africa can be sustained through in­
vestment initiatives between banks and the private sector. (iii) Africa’s 
development towards a low-carbon economy also requires structural 
diversification away from economic dependence on natural resources. 
Given that GSEs are more economically active in the resource-dependent 
industrial sectors (e.g. oil and gas, mining) in Africa and only play a 
minimal role in increasing output in the low-carbon, high-tech tertiary 
sectors, policy path II also becomes effective for supporting economic 
diversification strategies. This finding implies that banks, through 
financial intermediary interactions with the private sector, could limit 
the resources available for deepening dependence on natural resource- 
reliant sectors while also playing a key role in accelerating the devel­
opment of high-tech tertiary industries. 
The above policy considerations suggest that without appropriate 
bank credit allocation policies in Africa (for example, adopting Policy 
Scenario I instead of Policy Scenario II), PSUs will be deprived of 
funding, the eco-efficiency benefits of renewable energy will decline, 
and economic growth will become more carbon-intensive. Therefore, 
financing low-carbon growth in Africa requires private sector-focused 
policies. All African countries must shift from a government-driven to 
a market-driven policy environment and fully support the carbon 
reduction effect of market-driven credit resource allocation. To some 
extent, the political relationship between the GSEs and the government 
impedes the market selection effect on bank credit allocation decisions, 
which is indirectly reflected in the allocation of limited financial re­
sources to carbon-intensive sectors. It is essential to remove ownership 
discrimination from policy decisions and allow market forces to deter­
mine the direction of available financial resources. This will help banks 
develop healthily and improve the efficiency of financial resource 
allocation. 
Addressing the barriers to funding economic activity in the private 
sector will be a key priority for policymakers. There are two critical 
policy areas that require immediate action. One, the depth and breadth 
of the financial intermediary role of the banking sector in Africa is still 
weak and needs to be strengthened. This will undoubtedly require a 
range of policy measures to create a well-functioning banking system 
with an adequate financial intermediary infrastructure, including the 
integration of advances in information and communication technology. 
It would also necessitate building efficient and effective regulatory, 
legal, and institutional conditions, which are required to foster in­
novations that have the potential to improve resource mobilization and 
allocation mechanisms. In many African countries, the legal and regu­
latory conditions are not yet pro-business and investment-friendly, 
hampering financial intermediation and reducing PSUs’ capacity to ac­
cess bank credit resources. Two, the bank lending-deposit spread in in­
terest rates in Africa remains quite high, even higher than what is 
available elsewhere in the world. This reflects the high cost of credit 
resources and explains why the private sector continues to struggle to 
gain an economic foothold in Africa. This necessitates the development 
of innovative solutions. In this regard, policy measures may have to 
consider granting PSUs lower or favourable interest rates. Given that 
most low-carbon production projects lock-in financing costs over a long 
period of time, strategically selected low interest rates can help reduce 
financing constraints. Part of this strategy would require banks to 
develop green corporate finance packages. The incentive effects of bank 
credit policy on PSUs can help African economies build capacity in high- 
tech tertiary industries. With the inverted U-shaped curve between per 
capita GDP and CO2gdp, increasing PSUs’ investment in high-tech ter­
tiary industries can be an important source of structural transformation 
and can effectively improve production and energy efficiency, which are 
conditions needed to sustain growth in per capita GDP while inducing a 
reduction in CO2 emissions. 
The main limitation of this study is that the conclusion and policy 
recommendations are based on a panel analysis of 34 African countries 
and do not take into account the specifics of each country. In fact, there 
are some differences in the depth of bank funding in African countries. 
Therefore, it is important to extend this study at a country level to gain 
further insights. In addition, future research could reassess the economic 
efficiency potential of the two bank lending policy scenarios using an 
ecologically-adjusted GDP that would account for the use of key pro­
ductive resources such as land, forest, and water, which are not 
accounted for in the carbon intensity indicator used in this analysis.
