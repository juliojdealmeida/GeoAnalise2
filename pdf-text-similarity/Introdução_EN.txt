INTRODUCTION
Contemporary tourism has consolidated itself as a strategic vector for socioeconomic development and income decentralization in developing countries.
Especially in regions marked by the contraction of traditional industrial and agricultural activities, the transition to alternative tourism modalities, especially rural tourism, ecotourism, and agritourism, emerges as a viable alternative for diversifying the productive matrix.
As postulated by the local development literature, this segment differs from mass tourism by valuing community know-how, the authenticity of landscapes, and the intangible cultural heritage of each territory, promoting complementary occupations to family farming without erasing peasant identity. However, recent literature shows that the mere existence of natural or cultural attractions is not an automatic guarantee of regional development (CAETANO; WEGNER; XAVIER, 2023).
The operational success of a tourist destination depends on the level of organizational articulation and the territory's capacity to convert its assets into marketable and sustainable products.
Despite its significant potential for development, the expansion of rural tourism in Brazil occurs in a context of profound structural heterogeneity, a concept widely discussed in CEPAL literature to describe the coexistence of modern, highly productive sectors alongside subsistence pockets and scarce resources.
This disparity is reflected in the spatial distribution of support and credit resources across the territory.
While pole municipalities and regional capitals concentrate most private investment and large-scale infrastructure, small localities with a vocation for community ecotourism face serious financial liquidity constraints.
According to the UN Tourism global diagnosis (2023), the shortage of support infrastructure (such as telecommunications and transport) and limitations in access to formal financing mechanisms are the two main bottlenecks for sustainable tourism development in non-urban areas.
In this way, short-term financial support, theoretically interpreted as liquidity finance in the post-Keynesian view (ARAUJO, 2018), becomes the necessary oxygen to enable the first local structures and ensure the permanence of the population in rural areas.
OBS.: Araujo: finance (creation of liquidity to sanction investment decisions by agents) and funding (creation of financial instruments under conditions compatible with the maturity of the investment project).
Faced with this territorial mismatch, the present research proposes the formulation of the Synthetic Territorial Feasibility Index (STFI) as a geointelligence tool applied to tourism governance in the states of Bahia (BA) and Rio Grande do Sul (RS). The central objective is to analyze the degree of territorial readiness of municipalities for the development of community-based ecotourism, assessing to what extent the flow of financial capital in the field dialogues with productive supply and the digital attractiveness of destinations. The choice of these two federative units is justified by their markedly contrasting geographical and institutional profiles: Rio Grande do Sul has a consolidated history of rural routes and integrated cooperative credit, while Bahia has large-scale tourist poles concentrated on the coast but faces the challenge of internalizing tourism revenues toward the semiarid region and the Chapada Diamantina.
The STFI therefore aims to map these contrasts to support integrated public policy planning for tourism and development.
Methodology
The research adopts an analytical approach of applied and quantitative nature, using data from multiple official sources. The sample universe was delimited based on the focus of the scientific cutoff established by the guidelines of the Brazilian Tourism Ministry for the Tourism Map of Brazil, resulting in a final set of 420 target municipalities with vocation for ecotourism and community-based tourism: 136 municipalities in Bahia and 284 in Rio Grande do Sul.
Although the Cadastur and Airbnb databases register general tourism flow, their application in this research is geographically limited to the universe of target municipalities with active territorial and rural vocation. Treating them as proximity variables (proxies), the limitation of capturing the general infrastructure services is assumed, justified by the lack of perfectly segregated national microdata for the purely rural activity.

The choice of this transformation is based on the econometric guidelines of Gujarati and Porter (2011), which recommend the use of the natural logarithm as a corrective measure to mitigate heteroscedasticity and smooth pronounced asymmetries in cross-sectional socioeconomic distributions. In rural territorial data, the presence of pole municipalities with high concentration of beds and listings generates extreme asymmetries that would make comparative analysis of small municipalities unfeasible.
The addition of the unit constant (operationalized in Python through np.log1p) ensures preservation of existing null values in the database without generating mathematical indeterminacies, since ln(0 + 1) = 0. The transformation compressed the numerical scales without altering the ordinal hierarchy of municipalities, preserving analytical sensitivity for differentiating small rural destinations.

As Mendes (2026) and Malczewski (1999) point out, normalization converts raw attributes into standardized adimensional indicators in the continuous interval from 0.0 to 1.0, where 0.0 represents the lowest relative attractiveness/infrastructure and 1.0 the upper limit of performance.
Methodologically, normalization was calculated in groups by Federative Unit (groupby('uf')). This procedure is theoretically justified by the need to neutralize structural asymmetries among the state economies of Rio Grande do Sul and Bahia, in line with the theory of structural heterogeneity in CEPAL (PORCILE, 2011; PINTO, 1970). By rescaling the data relative to each state's limits, evaluation of territorial feasibility respects the specificities of each regional context without federative bias.
This allows us to affirm that the STFI maps the existence of territorial synchronies (where rural support coincides with mature tourism structures) and, above all, the mismatches. It identifies “financial gaps,” that is, municipalities with strong digital appeal and natural attractions but outside the lines of support and credit in the field.
What remains open: the index does not measure the impact of short-term extreme climate events (such as floods in RS or severe droughts in BA), even though the literature points out that these climate shocks generate strong volatility in the tourism and agricultural supply chain. It also leaves open the capture of private resources not monitored by the Central Bank or the Ministry of Tourism, as well as the impact of specific sectoral support directed toward large companies (such as the general lines of FUNGETUR).
The model is therefore useful for identifying territory readiness and financing gaps in rural tourism development.
